Nature, Up Close and Enormous

From a clump of weeds to a giant wave: three artists who paid close attention to the natural world.

The Large Piece of Turf, 1503 · Albrecht Dürer

Dürer painted a patch of ordinary weeds, with grasses, plantain, yarrow and dandelion leaves, in watercolor and gouache. In 1503, most artists painted saints and kings, and plants were just background. Dürer looked at a muddy clump of grass as if it deserved a portrait, and painted every blade. Nobody knows exactly why he made it, but it is one of the first pure nature studies in Western art.

Dandelions, 1867–68 · Jean-François Millet, pastel

Millet was a farmer's son from Normandy who became famous for painting peasants at work. Here he painted the humble dandelion at every stage, from bud to flower to the white "clock" of seeds, in soft pastel on tan paper. One writer thinks he may be comparing the dandelions to poor country people, whose lives pass as quickly as seeds on the wind. The next year, Millet won his biggest commission ever, 25,000 francs, to paint the four seasons.

The Great Wave off Kanagawa, c. 1831 · Katsushika Hokusai

Hokusai was about 70 when he made this print of fishing boats caught under a giant wave, with tiny Mount Fuji far behind. He later wrote that nothing he made before seventy was worth much, and that at a hundred and ten, "every dot and every line will be alive" (adapted). The deep blue ink was a new pigment imported from Europe, and the print was made from carved wood blocks, thousands of times.

Fun Facts

SubjectFun Fact
Dandelions twiceDürer painted dandelion leaves in 1503. Millet painted whole dandelions about 360 years later.
Not a tsunamiThe Great Wave is a huge ocean wave, and the boats are fishing boats.
Many namesHokusai changed his name about 30 times.
A small waveHokusai's whole print is only about 25 by 38 centimetres.
