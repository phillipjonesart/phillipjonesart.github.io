Stories of Indigenous Peoples Around the World

Three stories from Mexico, the Great Plains and West Africa: one told by Aztec artists, one painted by a visitor, and one cast by the artists of Benin.

The founding of Tenochtitlan · Codex Mendoza, about 1541

Aztec artists painted this page to tell how their capital began. According to legend, their god told a wandering people to settle where they saw an eagle perched on a cactus. They found it on a marshy island in a lake, and the blue lines show the water, cut by a cross into four districts, with the ten founding leaders sitting around it. In 1519, a Spanish soldier named Bernal Díaz saw the city and wrote (adapted): "And some of our soldiers even asked whether it was not a dream."

Catlin Painting the Portrait of Mah-to-toh-pa (Four Bears), Mandan · George Catlin, 1832

In 1832, the American artist George Catlin traveled up the Missouri River and painted the Mandan people, including this scene of himself painting the chief Four Bears. Five years later, smallpox, spread from a fur-trade steamboat, killed almost all the Mandan. Four Bears, dying, is said to have given a last speech, written down by a trader (adapted): "Ever since I can remember, I have loved the whites. I have never wronged a white man, and they have deceived me. Think of your wives and children, all dead or marked with the frightful marks of that deadly disease."

Plaque of an Oba on horseback with attendants · Edo artists, Kingdom of Benin, 1500s–1600s

Artists in the Kingdom of Benin, in what is now Nigeria, cast hundreds of brass plaques like this one to decorate the royal palace. They showed kings, warriors and foreigners, such as Portuguese traders. In 1897, a British military force burned Benin City and carried off thousands of these objects, which were sold to museums around the world. They are known as the Benin Bronzes, though most are actually brass, and many are now being returned to Nigeria.
